---chapter: the leaving---

"Give me a bottle, give me a cigarette — I'll leave when I leave, I have time. I don't want to spend the days ahead crying alone, unable to move forward."

I'm leaving for Singapore soon, opening the next chapter of my life. This isn't a grand or sweeping memoir — just an ordinary goodbye, and some thoughts about my life that I've been turning over.

---chapter: the coach---

When I talk with my life coach, Steven A. Martin, we often come back to the same question: how to actually live a full life. He told me his story. At fifteen he ran away from home in Ohio, made his way alone to Hawaii, lived in a treehouse in the jungle. He's been a surf instructor, a chef, an observer at an astronomical station, a visiting scholar at Peking University, and a university professor. He once walked with his teacher from Xi'an to India, retracing Xuanzang's pilgrimage. To someone like me — raised on the standard Chinese path — his résumé was almost violent in its strangeness. When I asked him what he'd actually learned, he answered me quietly: "A life shouldn't be lived inside a box."

---chapter: the box---

Steven's story cracked something open. I started reconsidering how I was actually spending my time on this planet — which is, after all, finite. While I'm still young, while I still have the energy and the runway, I want to test the shape of the life I actually want, instead of inheriting one. Unlock more of what's possible. Everybody calls me a shoe — but what if I want to be a purse?

I've been living inside a preset template my whole life. Hit the milestone, do the next thing, glide through to the end. After really thinking it over, I realized that's not the life I want. Or at least — not right now.

---chapter: not farewell---

So the title — "Not Farewell, Motiff" — the weight is on "Not Farewell," not on Motiff.

I'm leaving the company, the project, the city. But what I'm taking with me is more than what I'm leaving behind. The engineering rigor, the pressure of working alongside people who are visibly better than you, the experience of building with a team that took design seriously — none of that stays in the building. It's already part of how I work.

Going to Singapore isn't because I've figured out what comes next. To be honest with the box metaphor: I'm just trading one box for another. Bigger, stranger, still a box. The difference isn't the destination — it's that this is the first time I've **consciously chosen** the next box, instead of being placed in it. The first square I drew on the grid myself.

I'm also clear-eyed that I'm not Steven. I won't live in a treehouse. I won't walk to India. Most of us won't, and that's fine. The lesson isn't to copy the extreme version — it's to ask, every so often: am I doing this because I actually want to, or because the previous square automatically funneled me into this one? Know what the default is, and then decide whether to take the default route. That's enough.

This isn't a "Goodbye" because I don't think this is an ending. The late nights, the code we shipped, the product decisions we argued about — those leave traces, in me and in everyone else who was there. We'll find each other again, in different cities, different companies, different projects. The line stays open.
