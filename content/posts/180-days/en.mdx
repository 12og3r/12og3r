---chapter: 180 days here---

A note up front: what follows is just my personal view of Singapore.

180 days since I moved to Singapore, 180 days back at ByteDance. Not long. Long enough to have collected some observations, though.

---chapter: weather---

As someone from the north of China, I really like the weather here. I've always liked the tropics — the warm humid air, the option of going to see the ocean whenever — and Singapore matches that perfectly.

There's also a much more practical layer: *I finally get to wear a t-shirt year-round*. Back home, there's that stretch every year where you have to dig through a wardrobe for sweaters, try on the down jacket and see if it still fits, spend an extra ten minutes at the door before leaving the apartment. That was never a ritual to me. It was a hassle. Singapore's weather keeps that hassle outside the year entirely. Step out the door, one t-shirt, done — a pretty plain kind of liberation.

---chapter: rhythm---

The work rhythm here is healthier than I expected.

For comparison: my impression of how big tech in China typically runs was 11 AM start, two-hour lunch beginning at noon. Which sounds reasonable until you realize the "actually doing things" part of the afternoon gets sliced apart, real output gets pushed into the evening, and what counts as the end of the workday turns into something nobody can quite pin down.

Here it's 10 AM start, one-hour lunch, off by 7 PM. Same eight hours. The time structure is rearranged, and the whole day shifts forward by two hours. On paper it looks like a one-or-two-digit difference. Lived, it's two completely different lives.

---chapter: slow---

The general pace of life here is slow too. Nobody seems particularly in a hurry. The most immediate way I noticed is sound — you almost never hear a car horn. At first I thought drivers not honking was a coincidence; living here a while, I realized it's just the default. People smoking on the street are also rare.

Something else I noticed: people walking their pets all carry a plastic bag, and the moment the pet does its business they clean it up. I asked a Singaporean friend how everyone manages to be this consistent. He said it comes from a sense of responsibility and respect for their country.

You also see *SLOW* painted on the asphalt on a lot of roads, telling drivers to ease off. After a while, I started feeling like the sign wasn't only for drivers — the whole city seems to be saying the same thing. Rushing doesn't necessarily make you faster. *Slow is smooth, smooth is fast.*

---chapter: careful---

People here don't necessarily move fast, but they're careful.

The first time it really hit me was at a construction site. Before a truck drove off the site, they'd hose down all four tires with a water gun, then drive out. So the dirt wouldn't get carried out onto the street.

The estate I live in was getting an exterior repaint. During the day, the whole zone was neatly fenced off; after the workers clocked out, everything had been packed away. Tools gone. Tarps gone. Ground clean. When I got home in the evening, you couldn't tell anyone had been working there earlier.

Back home it runs the other way: faster is better. But chase fast long enough and you get pre-made, reheat-to-order food. Quick, sure. Doesn't taste the same.

Singapore can't really go fast either. Small population, expensive labor, no setup for racing things through. So *slow* here is partly something they picked, partly something they're stuck with.

Whichever half it is — after a while of living here, I'm used to it.

---chapter: worth it---

The city's landscaping is really well done — I hadn't expected this before coming.

Cost of living here is genuinely higher than in China. But after living here for a while, I've started to feel that it's actually *worth it*.

The most direct example is housing. Rent is much more expensive — but the kind of condo I live in almost always comes with a pool and a gym, and the landscaping is taken seriously. Trees pruned. Grass maintained. Not the perfunctory kind a property manager throws together. Every time I walk through it on the way home, the money doesn't feel wasted.

You probably couldn't replicate this kind of complex in Beijing — the weather wouldn't allow it. In a city that's summer year-round, the green just keeps growing.

---chapter: michelin---

The most unexpected food thing was the hawker centers.

A surprising number of stalls have Michelin recommendations, and the price is still hawker price — five to ten Singapore dollars a meal. Like Fangzhuanchang 69 in Beijing, the zhajiangmian place.

---chapter: not far---

Something I hadn't realized before coming: living in Singapore doesn't require cutting the cord to China.

Cross-border shopping is easy. Spend 299 RMB and sea-freight to Singapore is free. Rough timing:

1. Sea freight isn't particularly slow — about 10 days
2. Air freight if you want it faster — under a week

There's no shortage of Chinese supermarkets here — most of the snacks, condiments, household goods you'd buy back home, you can find an equivalent brand for.

Chinese chains are showing up in force. On the drinks side: Chabaidao, Chagee, Yidiandian, HEYTEA, Mixue have all opened up here, and Luckin came along for the coffee. On the food side: Tai Er, Zhang Liang Malatang, Mahua Lamian, Tanyu — and Haidilao goes without saying (it was Singaporean from the start anyway). Retail-wise, Miniso has plenty of stores too.

The Chinese stuff is closer than I'd imagined. In a way, over these 180 days I haven't fully "left China" — I changed cities, but most of my daily goods, tastes, and brands are still on the same track.

---chapter: to be fair---

A note before I close.

I'm not saying Singapore is better, or that China is worse. No country is the complete package — Singapore has its own set of problems too.

It's just that this rhythm, this state of daily life, fits me better right now. That's all.
