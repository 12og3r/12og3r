---chapter: speechless---

I was chatting online with a former coworker the other day. Nothing in particular — just catching up. Then out of nowhere he asked: "If you suddenly lost your job, would you be anxious?"

Of course I would, I said.

He paused, then asked: "Why would you be anxious?"

I had nothing.

It wasn't that I couldn't think of an answer. It's that there were too many — visa, income, age, level, what's on the resume, where peers have gotten to, what my parents would say. Each one rolled out so smoothly I realized those weren't actually what I was thinking right then. They were prepared answers, sitting in some default template, waiting to be called.

---chapter: borrowed---

After we hung up I kept turning it over.

A lot of my anxiety, it turns out, is borrowed. It floats around inside a kind of universal framework — what people in tech my age are supposed to worry about, which level you're supposed to hit by when, how long you can be away from a big-name company before your resume "breaks," what it means when peers reach a milestone before you do. I'd never actually deconstructed any of it as *me*. Never asked the inside: do you actually care about this, or were you just told you should?

What's more uncomfortable: I'd thought about this kind of thing before. Back then I told myself that leaving for Singapore was just swapping one box for another — bigger, stranger, but at least this time I picked it. I remember thinking that with a little pride.

But the long, fluent list that came out of my mouth when he asked *why* — it wasn't produced by me, alone, thinking from scratch. It was retrieved from templates I've been fed for the last ten years. I changed cities, changed companies, changed language. The templates came along untouched.

Picking a box isn't the same as leaving the box. Path dependence is a lot more stubborn than a single decision — it's the most familiar handle, the one I instinctively reach for the next time I'm at a fork.

---chapter: take it apart---

I tried laying the items out one by one and asking each: "if it were just me, would this still hold?"

Some collapsed immediately. *Should be at level X by my thirties* — but level was never the reason I do this. *Resume can't break* — there's no interviewer reading my resume right now. I'm playing interviewer for one who doesn't exist. *Engineers should move into management by 35* — I've never actually asked myself if I want to. The template did the answering for me, and converted *want* into *should*.

The ones that survive the questioning, those are the real anxieties. Far fewer. Much sharper.

---chapter: mine---

The anxieties that turned out to actually be mine mostly cluster around freedom — fear of losing the room to keep doing what I want to do, fear of getting carried along by an inertia I don't even believe in. The worst one: fear of looking back years from now and realizing I never actually chose any of it.

These are uncomfortable. But at least they're mine. I can answer them, and I can put them to work.

The borrowed ones can't, because they aren't questions. They're noise. Trying to answer them is like arguing with someone who isn't in the room.

---chapter: out---

I'm probably not walking out of this box any time soon. What I can do is this — every time the anxiety shows up, pause and ask: is this mine, or is the template being anxious on my behalf? Was that reaction me, or was it path dependence pulling the lever?

If I think it through and still go with the template, that's fine. The only difference is this time I knew what I was doing.
